# Rules for describing acts in your own words

**You describe what happens in a story, event by event, as acts: what is done, in a plain verb of your own choosing, who does it, and to whom.**
Your prompt names the files you may open: these rules, the story or stories you work on, and their list of events. Open nothing else.

## Reading

**Read the whole story before writing anything.**
Whether an act matters to the story depends on what comes after it, so you cannot judge it until you have read to the end.

**The events are listed for you; keep them.**
Write entries for every listed event, in the listed order, and only for the listed events.

**Each act in an event gets one entry.**
Most events narrate one act and get one entry.
Where an event's words narrate two or more distinct acts, give each its own entry, in the order the text narrates them.
A single act gets a single entry.

## Describing an act

**Say what is done with one plain English verb, in its base form: the commonest everyday verb that says it.**
Write `kill`, not `slay` or `slays`; `ask`, not `entreat`; `give`, not `bestow`.
Say what the one who acts does, in the active voice: if someone is robbed, the act is the robber's, and its verb is what the robber does; if the one robbed then does something, that is an act of its own, with its own verb.
Choose the verb for what is done, not for what it leads to, and not for the part it plays in the story.

**Then say it again in a short phrase of your own, up to six words**, for example `gives the boy a horse` or `asks the old woman for shelter`.

**Name who does it and who it is done to.**
The performer is the one who does the act; the undergoer is the one it is done to, or `-` if no one. Name them as the text names them; in a play, exactly as its cast list names them.

**Do not use any theory of story structure.**
Do not use words for kinds of story events or for parts characters play, such as hero, villain, helper, quest, trial, climax or resolution; describe acts in ordinary words.

**Mark whether each entry matters to the course of the story.**
An entry matters when something later in the same story depends on it: it causes or enables a later act, it answers an earlier act, or it opens or closes the story's action.
Write Y when it matters, and N when nothing in the story depends on it.

**An act only threatened or intended, and not carried out, is not that act.**
A threat is described as a threat, with the verb `threaten`; write the act that is actually done.

**Keep the text's order.**

## The format

**`Notes.txt` has one line per entry.**

```
A000 | ev 3 | sell | sells the cow at the fair | the old man | - | Y |   | "<the key words of the clause, quoted>"; <note>
A000 | ev 4 | buy | buys a cart with the money | the old man | - | N | ? | "<quoted words>"; <note>
```

The fields are: story, event number, verb, phrase, performer, undergoer (or `-`), matters (Y or N), a question mark if you are unsure, and a note that quotes the words of the text the entry rests on.
The story field is the story's identifier as your prompt gives it (a tale's, a fable's, or a play's, such as P17), never a speech number.
Where one event has two or more entries, each has its own line, and they share the event number.

## Your report

**End with a short report.**
List every place where a rule left you unsure what to write, with the story and event.
Do not summarize the stories.
