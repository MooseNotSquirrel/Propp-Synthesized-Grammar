# Who is the hero? Judged heroes against the counting rule

**Frozen 2026-10-12 at the owner's word, before any reader saw a story.** A departure is logged in `Heroes/KeeperLog.md`, not made here.

## The question

Every hero in this project so far was chosen by a rule: the character who takes part in the most events of the story's event list.
The rule fails where a reader would not: in tale 151 it names the Little Devil, not Shabarsha, and it splits the tragedies' heroes by a few events.
Propp defines the hero as the one whose fortunes the tale follows (p.36), which is a judgment about the whole story, not a count.
This study asks readers to make that judgment, measures how far two readers agree, and compares their heroes with the rule's.

## The material

**All the stories transcribed so far, read from the same event lists the rule counted:** the 45 Russian tales, the 198 fables with events (the 100 drawn less F261, whose event list names no events, and the 99 held out), and the 32 tragedies.
Each story is given as its identifier, its title, for a play its cast list, and its numbered events. Nothing names a hero.

## The readers

**Two fresh readers, A on Opus and B on Sonnet, blind to each other.** For each story, one line: the hero, named as the event list names the character; or `no single hero`, with the two or three characters the story divides between; a confidence, high, medium or low; and one short reason.
The question they are given: the hero is the character whose fortunes the story follows: whose want or misfortune sets it going, who acts toward its outcome or suffers it, and whom the telling stays with; not simply the one who appears most.

## The measures

A reader's hero matches another hero when the first word of the two names, articles and punctuation left out, is the same; two answers of `no single hero` match each other.
1. **Reader agreement**, per genre: the share of stories where A and B name the same hero.
2. **Rule against readers**, per genre: the share of stories where the rule's hero (any of them, where the rule named more than one) matches the hero both readers agree on, over the stories where they agree.

## The predictions

1. **The readers agree on the hero in at least 90% of the Russian tales.**
2. **They agree less often in the tragedies than in the Russian tales.**
3. **The rule matches the agreed hero less often in the tragedies than in the Russian tales.**

Each is judged on its own. **Reported, not judged:** the fables on every measure; the stories where the readers disagree, and those where they agree with each other but not with the rule; the share of `no single hero` answers per genre.

## What the result is for

The heroes both readers agree on replace the rule's heroes in the hero-dependent measures (the failed hero, the trickster, the mapping from acts to functions); stories where they disagree are reported as such, not resolved by the keeper.
